3. Review checklist

Coherence and fairness

  • The case is solvable from the cast characters' cards and evidence.
  • Every fact in the solution's timeline matches the cards (times, names, pouch colours, door details).
  • The alibi matrix holds: only the culprit has an unaccounted-for window.
  • No single card names or decisively points to the culprit. Note how many cards contribute partially.
  • The culprit's cards never describe the deed. Judge whether the culprit's vague "afternoon" line is too much of a tell.
  • Red herrings are only convictions about a murderer, motive, or flaw; each is clearly false given other cards and none implicates the real culprit.
  • Every living player has a unique evidence piece and a quest target held by someone who is present.

Fun

  • Each character has a quirk to lean into, props, and conversation starters.
  • Phase 1 secrets are low-stakes, funny gossip about other characters (mostly).
  • Master Crowe's death scene reveals the method clearly and is easy to act out.
  • Trading secrets and evidence feels rewarding in both phases.
  • Casting makes sense for the actual players (see the table in the host guide).
  • The invitation page's name form leads each guest to their own Phase 1 page; each character page unlocks its Phase 2 card with a passphrase the host reveals when Phase 2 starts (ask the host for it to preview).

Content and tone

  • Family-friendly: romance only, no affairs, no drug themes, drinking limited to mead, wine and cider.
  • The queen's homeland (far east) appears only on her own cards, her escort's, and in Phase 2 materials; the invitation never names it. Her portrayal is respectful, not stereotyped.
  • Medieval European feel: kings, knights, merchants, servants; anachronisms are playful.

Reporting

Reply to the host with problems or suggestions, without revealing the solution. If the host reports a cancellation, read the contingency section of the solution and advise how to cover the role.
